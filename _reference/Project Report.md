## 1. Executive summary

The paper proposes a way to **encrypt multiple color images at the same time**, with **compression built in**. The core idea: take each image, shrink it to 1/4 size using the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), stack all the shrunken images on top of each other into a 3D block called a **cube**, and then scramble that cube using a sequence generated by a **3D chaotic map**. The result is a noise-like cube that can be transmitted and decrypted on the other end with the right key.

**Three things make this paper noteworthy:**

1. It handles **multiple images of different sizes in one pass** (most schemes do one image at a time).
2. The reconstructed image quality is **PSNR 32.06 dB**, beating four similar schemes that landed at 26–27 dB.
3. It survives every standard security test: NIST randomness (15/15 pass), differential attack (NPCR 99.65%, UACI 33.49%), information entropy (7.9994/8), key sensitivity to 10⁻¹⁵ perturbations, shear attacks (15% cut), and noise attacks (salt-pepper + Gaussian).

**Honest limitation:** when images in the batch are different sizes, the cube has to be padded with zeros, wasting space. The authors flag this as future work.

**Take-home line:** *Compression and encryption combined in one pipeline, on multiple images at once, with strong security and near-lossless reconstruction.*

## 2. The problem

Sending color images across the internet has two simultaneous problems the paper tries to solve in one pipeline.

### 2.1 Transmission is expensive

A 512×512 color image has 786,432 pixels (3 channels × 262,144 pixels). At 8 bits per channel, that's about 0.75 MB uncompressed per image. If you're transmitting batches of images — medical scans, surveillance frames, satellite imagery — bandwidth gets expensive fast.

**Standard solution:** compress before sending. JPEG compresses 10:1 to 50:1. But JPEG by itself doesn't protect the data.

### 2.2 Transmission is unsafe

Anyone intercepting the data can see the image. If those images are sensitive — patient scans, ID photos, military reconnaissance — you need encryption.

**Standard solution:** encrypt before sending. AES is the gold standard. But AES by itself doesn't compress; you pay full bandwidth.

### 2.3 The double-pipeline cost

Most existing systems chain the two: compress → encrypt → transmit → decrypt → decompress. That's **two separate pipelines**, both costing CPU time and engineering complexity. Also, both run **per image**: if you have 10 images, you compress 10 times and encrypt 10 times.

### 2.4 What the paper aims for

A **single integrated pipeline** that:
- Compresses every image to 1/4 size,
- Handles **multiple images of different sizes** at once,
- Encrypts them **as one unit** (the cube), not individually,
- Recovers the originals with high quality,
- Resists all standard cryptographic attacks.

## 3. Background

If terminology like "DWT," "chaotic map," "confusion," or "diffusion" is new, the dedicated explainers in [`02 - Concepts/`](02%20-%20Concepts/) walk through each one from scratch. This section gives the minimum so the rest of the report makes sense.

### 3.1 What is the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)?

DWT splits an image into **four sub-bands**, each at half the original resolution:

| Sub-band | What it captures | Mental model |
|---|---|---|
| **LL** | Low frequencies in both directions | A small, blurry version of the image |
| LH | Horizontal edges | Vertical lines / horizontal change |
| HL | Vertical edges | Horizontal lines / vertical change |
| HH | Diagonal edges | Diagonal change / corners / noise |

The trick: **most of the image's perceptual content lives in LL.** The other three sub-bands hold edge details that, if discarded, leave the image still recognizable.

**Compression by throwing away three of four sub-bands:** keeping only LL gives you a half-height, half-width version → that's 1/4 the pixel count → 1/4 the size. The reconstruction won't be pixel-perfect (it's lossy), but it'll be visually close.

DWT is in the same family as the DCT we learned in Week 6 for JPEG. The difference: DCT is *global* in space (it sees the whole image as one frequency soup), while DWT is *localized* in both space and frequency. That makes DWT better at preserving edges.

See [`02 - Concepts/02 - Discrete Wavelet Transform.md`](02%20-%20Concepts/02%20-%20Discrete%20Wavelet%20Transform.md) for the full explanation.

### 3.2 What is a chaotic map?

A **chaotic map** is just a function `f(state) → new state` that you keep applying to a state vector. The sequence of states it produces looks random but is fully deterministic if you know:
- The function `f`,
- The function's parameters,
- The starting state.

Two important properties:

1. **Sensitivity to initial conditions** — change the starting state by 10⁻¹⁵ and after a hundred iterations the trajectory is completely different. This is what makes the starting values usable as an **encryption key**: any tiny change to the key produces a totally different key stream.
2. **Statistical randomness** — the sequence passes statistical tests for randomness (NIST), so it can be used like a one-time-pad without an attacker being able to predict it.

The paper uses a **3D discrete chaotic map**: it takes a state `(x, y, z)` and three parameters and produces a new `(x, y, z)`. Iterated, it produces three sequences `X`, `Y`, `Z` that are used to drive the encryption.

See [`02 - Concepts/03 - Chaotic Maps.md`](02%20-%20Concepts/03%20-%20Chaotic%20Maps.md) for the full explanation.

### 3.3 Confusion and diffusion (Shannon's two requirements)

In 1949, Claude Shannon defined what any strong cipher needs:

- **Confusion** — *the relationship between the ciphertext and the key is complex.* Practically, this means **rearranging where things are** (positions).
- **Diffusion** — *the influence of every plaintext bit spreads over many ciphertext bits.* Practically, this means **changing what things are** (values).

The paper uses both:
- **Confusion step**: scrambles pixel positions inside the cube using the chaotic sequence.
- **Diffusion step**: changes each pixel's value via XOR with the chaotic sequence.

See [`02 - Concepts/04 - Confusion and Diffusion.md`](02%20-%20Concepts/04%20-%20Confusion%20and%20Diffusion.md).

## 4. Novelty

The paper itself lists three innovations (Section 1, end):

> *(1) Multiple color images of different sizes are compressed using DWT, and the resulting compressed reconstructed images are visually clearer.*
>
> *(2) Compatibility is achieved by encrypting multiple color images of different sizes in a cube.*
>
> *(3) The performance and attack resistance of the encryption scheme were tested. The results indicate some advantages over other similar schemes.*

In plain English:

### 4.1 Encrypt the cube, not the images

Prior multi-image schemes encrypt each image separately, then concatenate the ciphertexts. This paper **stacks all images into a single 3D cube and encrypts the cube as one object**. The chaotic sequence indexes into 3D coordinates `(i, j, k)` instead of 2D coordinates `(i, j)`, which is what unlocks the multi-image-at-once property.

### 4.2 Multiple-image, multiple-size

Prior schemes that did encrypt multiple images at once required all images to be the **same size**. This paper handles **different-size images** by:
- DWT-compressing each one to its own LL sub-band.
- Padding smaller LL sub-bands with zeros to fit the cube dimensions (set by the largest image).
- Treating the resulting padded cube as the encryption target.

### 4.3 Reconstruction quality is genuinely better

The paper's Table 2 compares average PSNR after compress-encrypt-decompress against four prior chaos-based schemes. Their PSNR: **32.06 dB**. Best prior result: **27.69 dB**. That's a 4+ dB jump on a logarithmic scale — meaningful, not noise.

## 5. Methodology

The full pipeline has 4 stages. The diagram from the paper (Fig. 4) shows them as boxes; this section walks each stage.

```
[Input: N color images, different sizes]
        │
        ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 1: DWT compress  │  → Each R, G, B channel of each image → LL sub-band only
└─────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 2: Stack cube C  │  → All LL sub-bands stacked into a 3D cube
└─────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 3: Confusion     │  → Pixel positions scrambled using chaotic sequence (S1, T1, U1)
└─────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
┌─────────────────────────┐
│  Stage 4: Diffusion     │  → Pixel values XORed with chaotic sequence (X2, Y2, Z2)
└─────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
[Output: cipher cube D] → transmit
```

### 5.1 Stage 1 — DWT compression

For each input image and each color channel (R, G, B independently):

1. Apply DWT once → four sub-bands: `LL`, `LH`, `HL`, `HH`. Each is half the height and half the width of the original.
2. **Keep only LL.** Throw away LH, HL, HH.
3. Normalize the LL values into the standard pixel range (so they can be merged with LL sub-bands from other images).

After this step, each image is 1/4 of its original pixel count and is represented by three LL sub-bands (one per channel).

**Why LL only?** Because LL holds the low-frequency approximation — what your eye perceives as the image's content. The other three sub-bands hold edge details that, if discarded, still leave a recognizable image. The reconstruction PSNR of 32 dB confirms this works in practice.

### 5.2 Stage 2 — Build the plaintext cube C

Stack all the LL sub-bands into a single 3D array called the **plaintext cube** `C`.

| Dimension | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Width `M_w` | `w_max / 2` | Half the widest input image's width |
| Height `M_h` | `h_max / 2` | Half the tallest input image's height |
| Depth `z` | ceil(`P / (M_h × M_w)`) | Enough layers to fit total pixel count `P` |

Smaller LL sub-bands get **padded with zeros** to fit `M_h × M_w`. Each layer of depth represents one channel of one image (with padding as needed).

From `C`, the algorithm derives **plaintext parameters** (e.g., sum of pixels) that get folded into the encryption key — this makes the key **plaintext-aware**: a different input image produces a different key, blocking certain attacks.

### 5.3 Stage 3 — Confusion (scramble positions)

The goal: move pixels around in the cube so an attacker can't tell which pixel came from which image.

1. Iterate the chaotic map `M_h × M_w × z` times (once per cube pixel) → three sequences `X`, `Y`, `Z`.
2. Post-process each sequence with `floor` and `mod` to turn the real-valued chaotic outputs into integer coordinates: get `S1`, `T1`, `U1`, the **offset sequences**.
3. For every pixel position `(i, j, k)` in the cube, look up offsets `(S1(k), T1(k), U1(k))`. Compare each coordinate against its offset:
   - If `i > S1(k)` and `j > T1(k)` and `k > U1(k)`: swap position with `(i + S1(k), j + T1(k), k + U1(k))`.
   - If `i < S1(k)` and `j < T1(k)` and `k < U1(k)`: swap with `(|i − S1(k)|, |j − T1(k)|, |k − U1(k)|)`.
   - Other 7 boundary cases: defined in Equation 10 of the paper (mostly variations on the above; one case leaves the pixel in place).

After running for every pixel, the cube becomes the **confusion cube `C'`**: same pixel values, scrambled positions.

**Don't memorize all nine cases.** For the talk just say: *"There's a 3-way comparison per coordinate, giving 9 swap rules. The cleanest way to think of it: bigger means add the offset, smaller means subtract, equal means stay put."*

### 5.4 Stage 4 — Diffusion (change values via XOR)

The goal: change each pixel's value so the cube looks like noise even if positions were guessed.

1. Iterate the chaotic map another `M_h × M_w × z` times → second set of sequences `X2`, `Y2`, `Z2`.
2. Flatten `C'` into a 1D vector `V` of length `M_h × M_w × z`.
3. For the **first pixel** of `V`: XOR it with a seed value derived from `X2(0)`. Call the result `V'(0)`.
4. For **every subsequent pixel** `V(n)`:
   - Compute `n mod 3`.
   - If 0, XOR with `X2(n)`; if 1, XOR with `Y2(n)`; if 2, XOR with `Z2(n)`.
   - Also XOR with the **previous output** `V'(n−1)`. This is the critical part: it makes a single bit change cascade through the rest of the cipher.
5. Reshape `V'` back into a 3D cube → this is the **cipher cube `D`**, the final encrypted output.

The cascade property (chaining each output to the previous one) is what gives the scheme its avalanche behavior. Flipping a single bit anywhere in the plaintext changes every subsequent ciphertext pixel — this is why NPCR is 99.65%.

### 5.5 Decryption (reverse the process)

Decryption is just encryption run backwards:
1. Iterate the chaotic map with the same key → reproduce `X2, Y2, Z2` and `S1, T1, U1`.
2. **Inverse diffusion**: XOR each cipher pixel with the same sequences (XOR is its own inverse) to undo Stage 4.
3. **Inverse confusion**: walk the cube backwards from the last pixel and undo each swap.
4. The result is the plaintext cube `C`.
5. **Inverse DWT** each LL sub-band (with zero sub-bands for LH, HL, HH) to reconstruct each image.

Because LH, HL, HH were discarded, the recovered images aren't pixel-perfect — they're **visually identical** but with PSNR 32.06 dB (vs. ∞ dB for a perfect reconstruction). That loss is the compression cost.

## 6. Findings

### 6.1 Reconstruction quality (the headline number)

**Average PSNR across test images: 32.06 dB.**

Reference points for context:
- 50+ dB → indistinguishable from original
- 30–40 dB → visually very close, slight differences only under careful inspection
- 20–30 dB → noticeable artifacts but still recognizable
- < 20 dB → significant degradation

So 32.06 dB is squarely in the "visually very close" range — the recovered image looks the same to a human observer.

### 6.2 Comparison vs. prior work (Table 2 of paper)

| Scheme | Average PSNR (dB) |
|---|---|
| **This paper (DWT + 3D chaos)** | **32.06** |
| Ref. [8] | 26.29 |
| Ref. [19] | 27.49 |
| Ref. [22] | 27.69 |
| Ref. [46] | 27.34 |

4–6 dB advantage. Because PSNR is logarithmic (10 log of error ratio), that's roughly a 2.5–4× reduction in reconstruction error — a genuinely large gap.

### 6.3 Encryption speed (Table 10 of paper)

For a 20-unit workload where 1 unit = 256×256 pixels (i.e., processing the equivalent of ~20 small images):

| Stage | Time (s) | Throughput (MB/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Confusion | 0.1124 | 177.94 |
| Diffusion | 3.1381 | 6.37 |
| **Total encryption** | **3.2505** | **6.15** |

| Stage | Time (s) | Throughput (MB/s) |
|---|---|---|
| Inverse confusion | 0.1016 | 196.85 |
| Inverse diffusion | 3.1762 | 6.30 |
| Second inverse confusion | 0.6349 | 63.00 |
| **Total decryption** | **3.2778** | **6.10** |

The diffusion step dominates because every pixel needs at least one XOR with the chain. Confusion is much faster because it's just position swaps.

## 7. Security analysis

The paper runs six categories of test. Every single one passes.

### 7.1 Chaotic key quality (NIST randomness suite)

The chaotic sequence is fed to the **NIST Statistical Test Suite** (15 tests covering frequency, runs, FFT, entropy, complexity, etc.). Pass criteria:
- p-value ≥ 0.01
- Pass rate > 96%

**Result: all 15 tests pass.** This proves the chaotic sequence is statistically indistinguishable from true random data — an attacker can't predict the next bit.

### 7.2 Key sensitivity (Section 6.1.2)

Encrypt with key `x0 = 0.1`. Try to decrypt with `x0' = 0.1 + 10⁻¹⁵` (a perturbation in the 15th decimal). **The decryption fails completely** — the output is noise. NPCR between the two decryption attempts is > 99%, meaning the encryption is hypersensitive to its key. **Implication:** the only way to decrypt is with the exact key.

### 7.3 Differential attack (NPCR + UACI, Section 6.2.1)

The attack: encrypt the original. Flip one pixel. Encrypt again. Compare the two ciphertexts.

- **NPCR** (Number of Pixel Change Rate) — % of pixels that differ between the two. Ideal: 99.6094%. **Paper: 99.6533%.**
- **UACI** (Unified Average Changing Intensity) — average % difference in pixel value. Ideal: 33.4635%. **Paper: 33.4887%.**

Both metrics land right on the ideal targets. That means a one-pixel change in the input changes essentially every pixel in the output (avalanche), so an attacker can't extract structure by perturbing inputs.

### 7.4 Chosen-plaintext attack (Section 6.2.2)

Attackers sometimes try to break encryption by feeding it pathological inputs like an all-black or all-white image. The reasoning: if the encryption is weak, the output might still show structure.

**The paper tests this directly:** encrypting all-black and all-white images. Both ciphertexts look like uniform noise. No structure leaks. The decryption recovers the all-black / all-white image cleanly.

### 7.5 Statistical analysis

Three sub-tests:

**Histogram (Section 6.3.1):** Plaintext histograms are non-uniform (some pixel values are common, others rare). Cipher histograms are flat — every pixel value equally likely. An attacker analyzing the histogram learns nothing.

**Correlation (Section 6.3.2):** Adjacent pixels in plaintext are highly correlated (correlation ≈ 1). In ciphertext, correlation ≈ 0 in horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions. Adjacent-pixel attacks are blocked.

**Information entropy (Section 6.3.3):** The cipher images achieve entropy ≈ 7.9994 (theoretical max = 8 for 8-bit). That's basically perfect noise.

### 7.6 Robustness attacks (Sections 6.4–6.5)

**Shear attack:** Cut 15% of the cipher cube. Attempt to decrypt the rest. Result: images still recoverable with PSNR ~30 dB (Table 9 of paper). The scheme is **fault-tolerant** to transmission damage.

**Noise attack:** Inject salt-pepper noise (intensity 0.002) and Gaussian noise (intensity 10⁻⁶) into the cipher cube. Decrypt. Result: visually recognizable images despite noise. **Robust to transmission noise.**

## 8. Limitations

The paper is honest about its main weakness (Section 7, conclusion):

> *"For extra pixels in the cube, the scheme is padded with zeros, which takes up space, and there is a need to reduce the utilization of the extra space in future encryption schemes to make the new encryption scheme more robust and less resource-intensive."*

**In plain English:** when images in the batch have very different sizes, the cube has to be padded with zeros to fit the largest one's dimensions. Those zero pixels still get encrypted and transmitted, wasting bandwidth. If you have one 4K image and nineteen 256×256 thumbnails in the same batch, the cube is mostly zeros.

**Other honest limitations** (not in the paper but you should know):

1. **Not formally proven secure.** The security claims are empirical (passes NIST, NPCR/UACI on the ideal targets, etc.) but there's no mathematical proof of cryptographic strength, unlike AES which has years of formal cryptanalysis.
2. **Diffusion is the bottleneck.** 6 MB/s throughput is slow — AES does hundreds of MB/s. For real-time video this would be too slow.
3. **Lossy by design.** Throwing away LH, HL, HH sub-bands means perfect reconstruction is impossible. 32 dB is good for most use cases but not pixel-perfect (medical-grade reconstruction needs higher).

## 9. Applications

The paper points to scenarios where you batch-transmit multiple correlated images and need both bandwidth efficiency and confidentiality:

| Use case | Why this scheme fits |
|---|---|
| **Medical imaging** | Send a patient's scan series (MRI slices, CT batch) across hospitals encrypted in one shot |
| **Surveillance** | Archive multiple camera feeds together with built-in privacy |
| **Satellite/military** | Transmit reconnaissance batches over limited bandwidth |
| **IoT image sensors** | Sensor networks pushing batched snapshots upstream |
| **Cloud backup of photo libraries** | Compress + encrypt a roll of photos in one operation |

It is **not** a replacement for AES on a per-message basis, and it's not for video. Its niche is multi-image batched transmission with bandwidth constraints.

## TL;DR (memorize this paragraph)

This 2026 ACM TOMM paper proposes encrypting multiple color images of different sizes simultaneously by DWT-compressing each one to its LL sub-band, stacking them into a 3D plaintext cube, and then scrambling that cube via a 3D chaotic-map-driven confusion-and-diffusion pipeline. The novelty is treating the multi-image collection as one encryptable cube rather than as separate images. The reconstructed images reach 32.06 dB PSNR (vs. 26–27 dB for four prior schemes), and the scheme passes all 15 NIST randomness tests, lands on the ideal NPCR (99.65%) and UACI (33.49%) targets, achieves 7.9994 bits of information entropy (out of 8), and survives shear, noise, and chosen-plaintext attacks. The honest limitation is zero-padding waste when batch image sizes differ widely.
